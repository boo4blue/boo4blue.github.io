Why this exists

The manual work, removed

A certain game-show host builds brilliant games in a slide deck and does an absurd amount by hand. Every manual step below is a feature this platform removes. The full comparison is section 7 of the Plan.

One screen, pop-over drawers

No hopping between a board slide, a shop slide and a rules slide. Shop, quest, players, log, chat and the GM console slide over the board — the game never goes away.

The platform is the memory

Every entity keeps its identity, position, stats and history. Clones stay linked to their originals. Nobody forgets which goblin went where, because nobody has to remember.

Health, gold, points and items sit on an always-visible HUD. No player ever has to stop the game to ask what they're carrying.

The rig rules

1 · One fixed rig. One canvas size, one pose set, one anchor per slot — an item made for a slot fits that slot on every character.
2 · Fixed layer order. Feet, legs, chest, hands, head, accessory — bottom to top, always. A trash can worn as a helmet lands in the head slot, over everything below it.
3 · Pre-composed, not per-frame. When equipment changes, the dressed character is composed once into a single image. The board moves that one image.
4 · Nothing is lost. A wrong-slot drop is refused with feedback; unequipping returns the item straight to the backpack.
5 · Auto-fit applies here too. A new item image dropped in is fitted to its slot's anchor and scale automatically.

The GM's plug-in — assets, personas, actions, absolute rules

Two different things, shipped in that order: AI that makes pictures (bounded, reviewed) and AI that runs NPCs (scripted first, brains later). Both run through one narrow plug-in. The GM's own key, the GM's cost — no shared key is ever shipped.

5 · Absolute rules — exact, GM-set, unbreakable Enforced by the system

These are not instructions the AI is asked to follow. They are hard limits checked before everything the AI tries to say or do. If an action would break one, it does not happen.

Rule A — “Never leave the shop.” The merchant cannot wander off, be lured out, or be talked out of his room — by anyone, ever.
Rule B — “Never sell the cursed sword.” Not for any price, not to any player, not by accident. The sale is refused by the system before the AI can agree to it — which is exactly why the sword can sit in his stock safely. Try the button above.
Rule C — “Never attack unless attacked first.” The guard stays peaceful until the rule's own condition is met. A player's clever words cannot change that.

6 · The safety rails Around every NPC brain

Scripted dialogue first. Every AI attachment carries a fallback fixed dialogue — no key, no connection, AI off: the NPC never goes silent.
Action budget. Each AI gets a set number of actions per round, set by the GM. Budget spent, it waits — it cannot act without a turn.
Everything is logged. Every AI action and line lands in the event log, next to the dice and the ledger.
GM override, always. Pause, correct, or switch off any AI per-NPC from the console, mid-sentence if needed. The AI never outranks the GM.

The plan, in full

Sixteen sections, one north star

We are not remaking what a certain slide-deck game show does — we are making it better: easier, more convenient, more automatic. Where this plan states a fact, it is labelled a fact; where it states a target, it is labelled a target.

§0 How to read this plan

One screen at a time, everything full screen, drawers and sheets over the board. This revision folds in the owner's requirements, the Magic the Noah research (6 recent videos, 3 deep-dive board games, 37 frames), and an eight-team review. Gestures are verified by code path until the real-gesture gate in §13 passes on a real phone — nothing here claims otherwise.

§1 North star, non-goals & the Better-Than-Noah test

North star: the host of those game shows builds brilliant games and does an absurd amount by hand — he draws an asset then resizes it, hops between slides for board, shop and rules, changes health and gold by hand, and drags objects aside so players can look behind them. Every one of those manual steps is a feature this platform removes.

Non-goals: no full 3D rebuild, no native app, no paid AI as the default, no always-on video, no copying anyone's assets or branding — the format inspires original work only.

The acceptance test — 7 bars:

  • Turn resolution in one tap, including health and gold changes, with Undo.
  • Zero hand setup — Shop, Quest and Wheel work with no draw-then-resize, no clicking through screens.
  • Nested entry in one tap per level — Tavern, then Back Room, then Chest.
  • Equip in one hold or one drag — the equipped image auto-aligned on the moving character.
  • Reveal in one tap — a short-hold peek, or one tap on Reveal Behind.
  • Join friction, timed — a player joins by code or link from a phone browser; the join is timed and recorded.
  • Manual steps removed, counted — the manual baseline and this platform's count are both reported. No “better” claim without that baseline.
§2 The Interaction Law
  • Drag first. Pieces, cards, spaces and wheels are grabbed and moved directly. If it looks physical, it behaves physical. Buttons are the last resort.
  • Tap does the obvious thing. Tap a space to inspect it — or to enter it, if it has an inside. Tap the dice to roll. One tap, one clear result.
  • Hold opens options. On a phone, a long hold (550ms) raises a rectangular bottom-sheet menu — maximum 4 actions. The word “ring” is retired: a ring wastes a narrow screen and breaks the 44px rule at its edges. The sheet rises where the thumb already is. On a PC, the same hold becomes a right-click — a context menu at the cursor, with hover states and keyboard shortcuts around it.
  • Short-hold peek is separate. About 350ms lifts the object so you can peek behind it, then it settles back — no menu, no turn cost. Two gestures, two timings, never one gesture with two outcomes.
  • Almost no buttons. No buttons for anything a drag, tap or hold can do; controls live in drawers. Standing buttons are the view tabs, drawer handles, and form controls inside drawers.
  • No emojis. Anywhere. All icons and artwork are drawn illustrations made for this game.
  • Built for thumbs first. Every target at least 44px, nothing needs a mouse or a hover on mobile, drawers close with a swipe, a tap outside, or their Close button; safe areas respected on every phone.
  • One drawer stack, one back action. Drawers and sheets form a single stack with a close-first rule. The board is always underneath, one back action away.
§3 Views
  • The Board (home). The road fills the screen edge to edge. Drawers hold dice, shop, quest, players, the log and the GM console. Interiors reuse this same view — going inside a building changes the active room, not the app.
  • Drawers, not pages. Shop, Quest, Players, Log, GM Console, Chat and Voice slide over the board. You never leave the game to use them.
  • The Library. Full-screen preset browser. Hold a card to preview it; tap to load it onto the board.
  • GM Studio. Full-screen builder — drag spaces onto the road, rules into a stack, spin-test wheels on the same screen.
  • Wheels & Rules Lab. One large weighted wheel at a time. Flick it to spin. Every spin logged, weighted, replayable.
  • Inventory. A paper-doll drawer per player: character in the centre, six equip slots around it, backpack below.
  • AI. Where the GM plugs in a provider, approves asset drafts, writes personas, grants actions and events, and sets absolute rules.
  • Voice. Text chat in the drawer system plus room voice that follows the board's rooms. A persistent voice pill rides on every view.
  • Phones — the same game, one thumb. Nothing is cut on mobile. The board re-deals its 30 spaces into a tall portrait road so every space stays big enough to tap; drawers open as full-screen sheets; hold-menus rise from the bottom. On PC the same game spreads into a wide multi-panel table with a larger board canvas.
§4 Board and map model

Everything on the board is one kind of thing: a node in a nested tree — parent, children, position, visibility, owner. A space, a building, a chest, an NPC and a piece of loot are all nodes. Tap-in changes the active node; the board you see is always one node's children. Movement, collision and fog of war — a light radius that uncovers the dark, instead of hand reveals — are rules on nodes, enforced by the platform. Grow mode is first-class: a game can start from one square and grow space by space, the way the best of those slide-deck games do — except the platform remembers everything, so no goblin copy is ever lost track of.

§5 Nested containment — anything inside anything

Any node can hold any other node, to any depth: a building has an inside you go into; inside that inside there can be another building; a cardboard box can contain a whole second board. Deep copy-paste: copying something copies everything inside it; paste it anywhere and the whole nest comes with it, working exactly the same.

Design intent: infinite depth. The spec is unlimited — that rule is never rewritten as a small number. Ship cap: 3 levels (a target to raise): the first release ships tested to 3 levels. The test checklist proves depth 10 — cycles rejected, clone IDs new, paste limits held, undo working, phone load budget holding — and only then is the cap raised and re-proven. If depth 10 fails on a phone, the cap stays at 3 and the failure is reported, not hidden.

Rules that ship with the cap: cycles are rejected · deep copy issues new IDs for every node · clone size is logged · paste has a size limit · sync is delta sync plus an operation log, never whole-tree sync.

You always know where you are. Every node carries two addresses, both always visible: a human breadcrumb — Lantern Road / Tavern / Back Room / Chest / Lantern Oil — and a stable ID path — lantern_road / tavern_01 / back_room_01 / chest_03 / item_lantern_oil_01. Search works on both. A copy gets a “(Copy)” suffix and fresh IDs inside. Anything that loses its parent lands in the Orphans view instead of vanishing — the no-orphan rule. Try it on the Board: the Tavern is gold-outlined — go in, then deeper.

§7 GM tools — hold actions, rules, wheels, automation

GM hold actions (as data, not code). The GM attaches actions to objects from a fixed catalog — hold a tree to check behind it, a carpet to look under it, a safe to open it. For each action the GM sets whether it uses the player's turn, shown as a turn-cost pip in the hold sheet. The same engine that runs a player's hold action also runs automation — automation is the engine firing without a tap. The full Catalog A table lives in GM Studio.

Rules and events. “When X happens, do Y” rules fire on their own: land on Clone Land and a clone appears; end of round and the zombies advance; collect 3 flamingos and the win check runs. Every rule joins a living rulebook players can open at any time.

Wheels. Built-in, weighted, auditable — outcomes auto-apply (damage, gold, move, teleport); spent segments auto-remove and restore per the GM's rule; every spin lands in the full event log.

§10 Art and assets — the high-detail standard

Painterly, lit, textured, richly illustrated. The reference videos are the before-picture: flat shapes on gray slides, avatar cutouts dropped on top, toolbars visible. The after-picture is the same formats — winding roads, named spaces, shops, wheels, character figures — rendered at real illustration quality, in this palette: lantern gold, deep green, brick red, parchment.

The Lantern. Glass with a lit gradient and highlight streaks, a two-tone flame with a hot core, wood grain on the caps, a halo of light, drifting sparks.

A space tile: the Tavern. Timber over shaded plaster, a two-tone roof with an eaves shadow, lamplit windows, a hanging sign with the lantern mark, moonlight behind, a worn path to the door.

A character: the Tower Wizard. Layered robe shading with a fold highlight, a gradient beard, a starred hat, a glass orb with a specular highlight on a knotted staff. Full figures — never a flat circle with a face.

What counts as a pass: every tile and character has a clear light source with lit and shaded faces and at least one cast or contact shadow (flat single-tone fills fail) · materials read as materials — wood grain, stone, cloth folds, metal sheen (smooth plastic fills fail) · foreground, subject and background separable at a glance · full figures with faces, clothing layers and one signature prop · screens are designed compositions, not rows of text on gray · detail never costs clarity — a space's name and job are readable at phone size in one second.

Counts & the phone load budget (targets): 30 illustrated Lantern Road tiles · 4 player characters on the fixed rig plus a growing NPC/mob set · 10 wheel faces · shop goods, quest scroll and seal, dice faces, drawer handles, the lantern mark, interior tiles for every enterable building. The board's own tiles load first and must be playable before anything else arrives; interiors lazy-load on approach; a single nested room's images stay under a size cap; equipped characters travel as one pre-composed image. Deep nesting never means a phone downloads the whole tree to show one room. AI-generated art follows the same bar — a draft that fails it is rejected at the GM's review gate.
